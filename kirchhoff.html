Kirchhoff's Laws

Two rules. Any circuit you'll ever meet.

Kirchhoff's first law

What flows in must flow out

At any junction in a circuit, the sum of the currents flowing in equals the sum of the currents flowing out.

ΣIin = ΣIout
In other words: it is just conservation of charge. Charge can't be created or destroyed, and it can't build up at a junction – so every coulomb that arrives must leave.
Kirchhoff's second law

Every loop balances its energy books

Around any closed loop in a circuit, the sum of the e.m.f.s equals the sum of the potential differences.

ΣE = ΣIR
In other words: it is just conservation of energy. Each coulomb gains E joules in the cells and transfers IR joules in the resistors (V = W/Q). Back at the start, the books must balance.

"But I can already do circuits…"

True – for simple circuits. The second law only shows its power once cells sit in different branches.

ONE CELLYou don't need itCombine resistors in series and parallel, then use V = IR. Kirchhoff's second law gives the same answer and feels like extra work.
TWO OR MORE CELLSThe usual rules collapseResistors are no longer in series or in parallel. The "combine and divide" toolkit has nothing to grip.
KIRCHHOFFWrite, solve, doneOne equation per junction, one per loop → simultaneous equations → every current, including its direction.

The picture to keep in your head

Think of electric potential as height on a hill walk. A cell lifts every coulomb up by E joules; each resistor lets it back down by IR joules. Walk round any closed loop and you arrive back at your starting height – so all the ups and downs must cancel. That is all the second law says.

Junction X never stores charge

Why it must be true

Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = ΔQ/Δt. Charge can't be created or destroyed, and it can't pile up at a junction – so every coulomb arriving per second must leave per second.

You've used it already

  • Series: the current is the same everywhere – there are no junctions, so there is nowhere else for charge to go.
  • Parallel: the branch currents add up to the supply current. That's the first law at the junction where the branches split.

Exam habits

  • Draw and label an arrow for every unknown current before writing anything.
  • Unsure of a direction? Just guess. A negative answer simply means it flows the other way.
  • Use the first law to cut unknowns: writing I₃ = I₁ + I₂ leaves two unknowns, not three.
Kirchhoff's second law is conservation of energy. Potential difference is energy per coulomb (V = W/Q). Round any closed loop, the energy each coulomb gains from cells (ΣE) equals the energy it transfers in components (ΣIR).
Step 1

A single loop – honestly, a bit pointless

Walk clockwise round the loop from the bottom-left corner, adding up every change in potential:

+12 − 4I − 2I = 0
12 = 6I  ⇒  I = 2.0 A

You knew that already: Rtotal = 6 Ω, so I = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A. If this were all Kirchhoff's second law ever did, ignoring it would be fair enough.

So why bother? Because real networks have e.m.f.s in more than one branch. Then the series/parallel rules collapse – and Kirchhoff doesn't even blink.
Step 2

Two loops, three cells – where Kirchhoff earns its keep

Change any e.m.f., flip any cell, change any resistor. Every current is recalculated from just three equations.

Sign rules for walking a loop
