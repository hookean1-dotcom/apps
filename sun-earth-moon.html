How the model works

Sizes and distances in the diagram are not to scale. At true scale the Moon would sit about thirty Earth diameters from the Earth, and the Sun would be nearly four hundred times farther away than the Moon. What the diagram does get right is direction: where the Sun and the Moon lie as seen from the Earth, which way the Earth’s axis points and how far the Earth has turned, all worked out for the date shown.

The part of the Moon’s path that lies above the plane of the Earth’s orbit is drawn as a solid line and the part below it is dashed. The two small rings where the line changes are the nodes. The red dot on the Earth marks the United Kingdom.

Why the Moon is awkward

The Earth, the Moon and the Sun all pull on one another at once, and that three-body problem has no tidy formula. With only the Earth to think about, the Moon would follow one fixed ellipse for ever. The Sun’s pull keeps stretching that ellipse and swinging it round, so the Moon’s position cannot be written as a single equation.

The practical answer is to start from steady, average motion and then add a long list of small corrections. Each correction is a sine wave with its own size and its own period.

Four angles that keep time

Every correction is built from four angles, each of which grows at an almost constant rate.

Two slow drifts

The line joining the two nodes slides backwards, taking 18.61 years to go once round. The perigee creeps forwards and goes once round in 8.85 years. The Moon’s orbit is tilted to the Earth’s by 5.1° on average.

That tilt is why there is not an eclipse every month. At most new and full moons the Moon passes above or below the line from the Sun through the Earth. An eclipse needs a new or full moon close to a node, and that only happens during two eclipse seasons a little under six months apart. You can see the seasons as clusters of red marks on the year line.

The Saros

The four angles never all return to their starting values together, but three of them come very close.

223 synodic months6585.32 days
242 draconic months6585.36 days
239 anomalistic months6585.54 days

After 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours the Moon has almost the same phase, the same position relative to its node and the same distance from the Earth, so an eclipse is followed by a very similar one. The extra 8 hours means the Earth has made a third of a turn more, so the next eclipse in the series is seen about 120° of longitude farther west.

How accurate is it?

The model uses the 60 largest terms for the Moon’s longitude and distance and 36 for its latitude, from Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Checked against a reference ephemeris for the years 1900 to 2100, the Moon is always within 0.01° of the right place, which is a fiftieth of its own width in the sky, and its distance is right to about 12 km. Eclipse times agree with published tables to within a minute or two.

Professional lunar theories go much further. They use many thousands of terms and are tuned against laser measurements to the reflectors that the Apollo astronauts left on the Moon.
