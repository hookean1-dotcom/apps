Method you are following

A cell drives a current through a rheostat. A moving-coil ammeter in series reads the current, and a moving-coil voltmeter across the cell reads the terminal pd. Changing the rheostat changes both.

A cell of emf ε and internal resistance r gives a terminal pd of V = ε − Ir. A graph of V against I is therefore a straight line falling with gradient −r and intercept ε on the V axis.

This one is different from the practicals you have done before. The quantity you are really after — the emf — is not the gradient. It is where the line crosses I = 0, which is a current you never actually pass. You have to extend the line back to get it, and both of your lines have to be extended, not just the best one.
Reading the meters. Both are analogue and both needles drift by roughly a division while you watch them, so take the uncertainty as one whole division on each: ±0.02 A on the ammeter and ±0.02 V on the voltmeter. That gives every point an uncertainty in both directions, so your points get error boxes rather than plain bars.

Taking the readings

Move the rheostat, let the needles settle, then read both meters and record them. You need at least six settings, spread across the whole range the rheostat gives you.

Rheostat —
Take the reading where the needle sits, to the nearest division. On the ammeter one division is 0.02 A; on the voltmeter one division is 0.02 V. Look straight on — the mirror strip under the scale is there so you can line the needle up with its own reflection and kill the parallax.

Plotting V against I

Plot your points, then draw the line of best fit and the worst acceptable line. Both must be extended back to the V axis.

Your x axis has to start at I = 0. The emf is the intercept there. Start the axis at your smallest current instead and the line has nowhere to be extended to, and the emf cannot be read off at all.
