Sidereal and synodic days

Why does Earth take 23 h 56 min to spin once, yet the Sun takes 24 h to come back round? Watch Earth spin and orbit at the same time to find out.

Relative to the stars → sidereal Relative to the Sun → synodic (solar)
Measured against the stars

Sidereal day

23 h 56 min 4 s

The time for Earth to turn once – a full 360° – relative to the distant stars. A star that is due south tonight will be due south again one sidereal day later.

From the Latin sidus, “star”.

Measured against the Sun

Synodic (solar) day

24 h 00 min 00 s

The time from one local noon to the next – from the Sun being at its highest point in the sky until it is there again. This is the “day” our clocks are built around (on average).

From the Greek synodos, “meeting” – the observer and the Sun line up again.

The key idea

Earth does two things at once: it spins on its axis and it travels around the Sun. Both happen in the same direction (anticlockwise, seen from above the North Pole).

In one day Earth moves about 1° along its orbit (360° ÷ 365¼ days ≈ 0.986°). So after one complete turn relative to the stars, the Sun is not quite back overhead. Earth has to turn roughly 1° more – which takes about 4 minutes – before the Sun returns to the same place in the sky.

  • Compare the 6-day and 24-day years. The longer the year, the less Earth moves round its orbit each day – so the smaller the gap between the two kinds of day.
  • Switch to the real year. Now the difference is only 0.986° per day – far too small to see in one day. Speed up and watch the star clock slowly pull ahead of the Sun clock by about 4 minutes each day.

Where do the 4 minutes come from?

  1. Earth goes 360° round the Sun in 365.26 days, so each day it moves 360° ÷ 365.26 ≈ 0.986° along its orbit.
  2. To bring the Sun back overhead, Earth must therefore turn 360° + 0.986° = 360.986° in one solar day of 24 h.
  3. So Earth spins at 360.986° ÷ 24 h ≈ 15.04° per hour. Turning 1° takes 24 × 60 min ÷ 360.986 ≈ 3.99 min.
  4. The extra 0.986° takes 0.986 × 3.99 min ≈ 3 min 56 s. A sidereal day (just 360°) is therefore 24 h − 3 min 56 s = 23 h 56 min 4 s.

Counting the turns in a year

In one year (365.26 solar days), Earth spins 366.26 times relative to the stars. Going once round the Sun “uses up” one turn, so we see one fewer sunrise than star-rises.

Try it: walk once around a table while always facing its centre. You return having turned through 360° – without ever “spinning” relative to the table.

So a sidereal day = 24 h × 365.26 ÷ 366.26 = 23.934 h = 23 h 56 min 4 s.

For any planet that spins the same way it orbits:

1/Tsolar = 1/Tsidereal − 1/Torbit

If the spin is the opposite way to the orbit (retrograde, like Venus) the minus becomes a plus.

Why it matters

  • Stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night. That adds up to about 2 hours a month, which is why Orion is a winter constellation and is lost in the Sun's glare by summer.
  • Astronomers keep sidereal time. A star returns to the same place in the sky every sidereal day, so telescopes track at the sidereal rate.
  • Geostationary satellites orbit with a period of one sidereal day (23 h 56 min), not 24 h, so they stay above the same point on the spinning Earth.
  • The 4-minute daily gap adds up to exactly one whole day per year.

The fine print

Astronomers usually define the sidereal day using the position of the vernal equinox rather than a particular star. Because Earth's axis slowly wobbles (precession, a 26 000-year cycle), the equinox drifts very slightly. The sidereal day is 23 h 56 min 4.091 s, while one turn relative to the truly distant stars (the stellar day) is about 0.008 s longer.

So far we have said a solar day is 24 hours. That is only true on average. Measured with a sundial, the real Sun's day is sometimes a little longer and sometimes a little shorter. This is why astronomers distinguish two kinds of solar time.

Follows the real Sun

Apparent solar time

Time told by the actual Sun – the time a sundial shows. Local apparent noon is the instant the Sun crosses your meridian (due south in the UK), when it is highest in the sky.

The interval between apparent noons – the apparent solar day – changes slightly through the year, by up to about half a minute.

Follows an imaginary Sun

Mean solar time

Imagine a “mean Sun” that moves at a perfectly steady rate along the celestial equator, taking a year to go round. Its day – the mean solar day – is always exactly 24 h. Clocks keep mean solar time.

Greenwich Mean Time is mean solar time on the Greenwich meridian. Elsewhere, local mean noon is 4 minutes later for every degree west – the same “1° ≈ 4 min” as before. Monmouth (2.7° W) has local mean noon at about 12:11 GMT.

Length of the apparent solar day compared with 24 h, through the year. Move over the graph to read values.

Cause 1 · Earth's elliptical orbit

Earth moves fastest when closest to the Sun (perihelion, early January) – about 1.02° per day, compared with 0.95° per day in early July. A bigger step along the orbit means a bigger extra turn is needed, so January's solar days are longer.

Cause 2 · Earth's axial tilt (23.4°)

The Sun's yearly path (the ecliptic) is tilted to the equator. Near the solstices the Sun's daily shift is parallel to the equator and counts fully; near the equinoxes it is at an angle, so less of it counts. Solar days are longer near the solstices and shorter near the equinoxes.

Both causes make days long in late December, so that is when the longest apparent solar days occur (almost 24 h + 30 s). Values here use a standard approximation and are good to within a few seconds.

Sundial versus clock

Those few seconds a day add up. Over weeks, the real Sun drifts up to about a quarter of an hour ahead of or behind the mean Sun. The difference is called the Equation of Time.
