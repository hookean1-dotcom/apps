Temperature vs internal energy

Internal energy is the total energy stored by the particles of a system: the kinetic energy of their movement plus the potential energy stored in the bonds and forces between them.

Temperature is linked to the average kinetic energy of the particles. Heating a substance either raises its temperature (more kinetic energy) or changes its state (more potential energy), but not both at once.

A bath at 40 °C has far more internal energy than a cup of tea at 90 °C because it contains many more particles.

Specific heat capacity

ΔE = m c Δθ

The specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. Unit: J/kg °C (equivalently J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, because a change of 1 °C is a change of 1 K).

Water's value, about 4200 J/kg °C, is unusually high, which is why it is used in radiators and why the sea warms more slowly than the beach.

Specific latent heat

E = m L

The specific latent heat L is the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature. Unit: J/kg.

  • Fusion (solid ↔ liquid): water 334 000 J/kg
  • Vaporisation (liquid ↔ gas): water 2 260 000 J/kg

Vaporisation needs more energy because the particles must be separated completely, not just loosened.

Reading a heating curve

On a graph of temperature against time for a steady heater:

  • Sloping sections: one state is warming. A shallower slope means a larger heat capacity (mc).
  • Flat sections: the state is changing. The length of the plateau × power = energy for the change of state.
  • The gradient Δθ/Δt equals P ÷ (m c), so c = P ÷ (m × gradient).

On a cooling curve the plateau is where the substance freezes, releasing latent heat, which holds the temperature steady.

The required practical

  • Measure the mass of the block on a balance.
  • Put the heater and thermometer in the holes; add a drop of oil or water to the thermometer hole for good thermal contact.
  • Wrap the block in insulation.
  • Record the joulemeter reading (or V, I and t) and the temperature at regular intervals.
  • Plot θ against E. Gradient = 1 ÷ (m c), so c = 1 ÷ (m × gradient).

Why the result is usually too high: some energy heats the heater, the thermometer and the surroundings, so less than the measured E actually goes into the block. Try the None insulation setting and compare.

Why ignore the start of the graph: the heater has to warm up before energy flows steadily into the block.

Method of mixtures

energy lost by hot object = energy gained by cold object
m₁c₁(θ₁ − θ) = m₂c₂(θ − θ₂)

If ice is added, it first warms to 0 °C (m cice Δθ), then melts (m Lf), and the meltwater then warms (m cwater Δθ). Each stage is a separate term.

Teacher notes

  • The Heating lab models a heater element with its own heat capacity, so the warm-up lag seen in real data appears naturally.
  • Heat loss to the room is proportional to (θ − 20 °C), so losses grow as the sample heats and curves bend. Compare the gradient early and late.
  • The particle view switches between lattice, liquid and gas behaviour in proportion to how much has melted or boiled.
  • Practice questions use the "write equation → substitute → rearrange" method. They never use formula triangles.
