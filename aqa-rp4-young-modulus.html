Method you are following

A long wire is clamped at one end and run horizontally over a pulley at the edge of the bench, with a hanger on the free end. A marker is fixed to the wire part-way along and carries a vernier scale that slides against a fixed millimetre rule.

Loading the wire stretches it. With stress σ = F/A and strain ε = e/L, the Young modulus is E = σ/ε = FL/Ae. Plotting extension against force gives a gradient of L/AE, so E = L / (A × gradient).

Two things that will cost you marks if you rush. The extension is the change in the marker’s position, so you need a reading with no load on before you start. And the gradient comes out in millimetres per newton, which is not what the equation for E wants.

Plotting e against F

Choose your own scales, plot your points, then draw the line of best fit and the steepest or shallowest line that still passes through every error bar.

Every error bar is the same height. The vernier reads to the same 0.1 mm whatever the load, so ±0.1 mm is the same absolute amount everywhere on the graph — but it is a far bigger fraction of the small extensions at low load. The steepest and shallowest lines you can draw are set by the bars at the two ends, which is why loading the wire over as wide a range as it will safely take tightens your uncertainty in the gradient.
