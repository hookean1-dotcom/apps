Key ideas (4.11–4.13)

What causes the seasons?

The Earth's axis is tilted 23.5°. As the Earth orbits the Sun this tilt stays pointing the same way, so each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year (summer) and away for the other half (winter). It is not caused by distance — Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.

Solstices (4.12)

At the June solstice the north pole leans most towards the Sun: the Sun is highest at noon, days are longest, and the Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer. At the December solstice it's the opposite — lowest Sun, shortest days.

Equinoxes (4.12)

At the equinoxes (March & September) the axis leans neither towards nor away from the Sun. Day and night are equal (~12 h) everywhere, the Sun rises due east and sets due west, and it is overhead at the equator.

The Sun's changing path (4.13)

Through the year the Sun's noon height rises and falls, and its rising point swings between north-east (summer) and south-east (winter). Day length varies with it — longest at the summer solstice, shortest at the winter solstice.

Extremes of latitude

At the equator the Sun is always high and days stay near 12 h. At the poles there are six months of daylight and six of darkness. The Arctic/Antarctic Circles mark where the midnight Sun and polar night begin.
