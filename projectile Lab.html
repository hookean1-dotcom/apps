The big idea

Once launched, and ignoring air resistance, the only force on a projectile is its weight. So:

  • Horizontally: no force, so no acceleration. The horizontal velocity ux stays constant.
  • Vertically: constant acceleration g downwards, so use the SUVAT equations.
  • The two motions are independent. The only thing they share is the time.

Try Drop vs throw in the lab: a ball thrown sideways lands at the same moment as one dropped from the same height.

Resolving the launch velocity

ux = u cos θ
uy = u sin θ

Treat ux and uy separately, then recombine at the end with Pythagoras:

v = √(vx² + vy²)    angle = tan⁻¹(vy / vx)

Standard results (level ground)

time to top: t = u sin θ ÷ g
max height: H = (u sin θ)² ÷ 2g
time of flight: T = 2u sin θ ÷ g
range: R = u cos θ × T

Range is greatest at 45°. Angles that add up to 90° (e.g. 30° and 60°) give the same range. Derive these from SUVAT rather than memorising them.

Launched horizontally from a height h

vertical: h = ½ g t² → t = √(2h ÷ g)
horizontal: R = u t

The fall time depends only on the height, not on how fast it is thrown.

At the top of the flight

  • The vertical velocity is zero.
  • The horizontal velocity is not zero. It is still u cos θ, so the speed is at its minimum, not zero.
  • The acceleration is still g downwards, as it is everywhere along the path.

Air resistance

Drag acts opposite to the velocity and grows with speed (roughly ∝ v² for a ball). It reduces both components, so:

  • the range and maximum height are smaller
  • the path is not symmetrical: the way down is steeper than the way up
  • the best launch angle is less than 45°

A shot put barely notices drag; a table-tennis ball is dominated by it. Try both in the lab.

Teacher notes

  • Without air resistance, paths are calculated exactly. With drag, a small-step numerical method is used with F = ½ρCdAv².
  • Challenge levels reward calculating before firing. "Show the physics" gives a full worked solution.
  • The SUVAT solver always chooses the equation that leaves out the unwanted quantity, and shows the substitution and rearrangement.
  • No calculus is used anywhere: gradients and areas under graphs only.
