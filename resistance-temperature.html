The model

  1. A metal is a lattice of positive ions surrounded by free (delocalised) electrons.
  2. A potential difference makes the free electrons drift through the wire – this is the current.
  3. The electrons collide with the ions. These collisions oppose the flow of charge – this is resistance.
  4. Heating the wire gives the ions more energy, so they vibrate with a larger amplitude about their fixed positions.
  5. The electrons collide with the ions more often, so the current is smaller for the same p.d. – resistance increases.

Try this

  • Turn on Show vibration range. Compare the gaps between ions at −195 °C and at 350 °C.
  • Follow one electron at each temperature. How often is it knocked off course?
  • Record R every 50 °C and plot the graph. Describe the pattern.

Watch out in exams

  • Say ions, not atoms or particles. The ions stay in place – they do not move along the wire.
  • It is the ions that vibrate more, not the electrons.
  • Collisions transfer energy from the electrons to the ions – this is why a current heats a wire.
  • A thermistor behaves the opposite way: its resistance decreases as temperature rises.

Model notes: vibrations are greatly slowed down and enlarged so you can see them. Real ions vibrate about 10¹³ times per second. The wire behaves like copper (R = 5.0 Ω at 20 °C).
