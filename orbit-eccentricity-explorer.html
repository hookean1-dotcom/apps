About this explorer

An interactive laboratory for orbital eccentricity — the single number that says how far an orbit departs from a perfect circle.

The geometry

Every bound orbit is an ellipse with the central body at one focus (Kepler's 1st law). The shape is set by e:

  • r(ν) = a(1 − e²) / (1 + e·cos ν)
  • Perihelion q = a(1 − e), aphelion Q = a(1 + e)
  • Axis ratio b/a = √(1 − e²) — surprisingly close to 1 even for large e. An orbit at e = 0.5 is still 87% as "tall" as it is "wide"; what really moves is the focus, not the outline.

Why the drawing is set up this way

The central body stays pinned at the focus and the semi-major axis a is held constant as you drag. That is the physically meaningful comparison: same orbital energy, same period — only the shape changes. Because the ellipse's full width is always 2a, nothing ever grows off-screen; instead the orbit slides so the body swings far out on one side and skims close on the other.

The faint dotted circle is the circular orbit of the same semi-major axis (radius a, centred on the star) — the e = 0 reference the real orbit is being compared against.

Escape trajectories (e ≥ 1)

At e ≥ 1 the orbit is open and a is no longer meaningful, so that mode instead holds the perihelion distance fixed and draws the hyperbola. Interstellar visitors such as 1I/ʻOumuamua, 2I/Borisov and 3I/ATLAS live here.

Motion

The moving body obeys Kepler's equation M = E − e·sin E, solved by Newton–Raphson each frame, so it genuinely races through perihelion and crawls at aphelion. Turn on Equal areas to see Kepler's 2nd law: every wedge is swept in the same amount of time.

Keyboard

Data

Eccentricities are osculating orbital elements (J2000 where applicable) drawn from the NASA/JPL Small-Body Database, the JPL planetary fact sheets and the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Comet and exoplanet elements are refined over time, and highly perturbed bodies (sungrazers, interstellar objects) vary between epochs — values here are representative, not survey-grade.
