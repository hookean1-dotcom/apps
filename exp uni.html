What to notice in the Big Bang model

  • Rewind and everything converges — but it converges everywhere at once, not onto one point on the screen.
  • The galaxy count is fixed. They only get further apart, so density falls.
  • Below a ≈ 1/1100 the screen turns to glowing fog: the universe was an opaque plasma.
  • Galaxies age together — the early universe looks blue, small and clumpy.

What to notice in the Steady State model

  • It expands too — the galaxies still separate, and you still measure Hubble's law.
  • But new matter is continuously created in the gaps, so the density readout never moves.
  • There is no first moment. Slide as far back as you like; the picture is unchanged.
  • There is always a mixture of young and old galaxies — the universe never "looked different".

Why this matters

Expansion alone does not prove the Big Bang. Hoyle, Bondi and Gold accepted Hubble's observations completely and still built a universe with no beginning.

To choose between them you need evidence about the past: the microwave background, the amount of helium, and the fact that distant galaxies genuinely look different from nearby ones.

The two pictures are the same universe. Only the choice of "who is standing still" differs — and that choice is arbitrary. This is what the balloon gets right: on the surface of a balloon, no dot is the centre.
The exam sentence Galaxies are not flying through space away from a central explosion. Space itself is expanding between them, so every observer, everywhere, sees the same pattern of recession. The Big Bang happened everywhere at once — including where you are sitting right now.

The points fall on a straight line through the origin: a galaxy twice as far away recedes twice as fast. That is exactly what uniform stretching of space produces — and it is what both models predict.

t ≈ 1/H0 = 1 / (70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹) ≈ 4.4 × 1017 s ≈ 14 billion years Convert Mpc to km first: 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 1019 km. Running the expansion backwards at a constant rate gives the Hubble time — an upper estimate of the age of the universe.

The real value from ΛCDM is 13.8 billion years; the near-agreement with 1/H₀ is a coincidence of deceleration by gravity being roughly cancelled by later acceleration from dark energy.

Trap — this is where marks are lost Redshift on its own is evidence for expansion, not for the Big Bang. A Steady State universe expands as well, and produces an identical Hubble diagram. If a question asks for evidence for the Big Bang specifically, you must reach for the cosmic microwave background.
Part 4 — Choosing a winner

The evidence that decided it

Between about 1948 and 1965 the two models were genuine rivals. Three lines of evidence settled the argument — and all three are about what the universe was like in the past.

The cosmic microwave background

1 · Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)

If the universe began hot and dense, it was an opaque plasma for its first ~380 000 years. When it cooled to about 3000 K, electrons and protons combined into neutral hydrogen and the universe became transparent. The light released then still fills space — stretched by expansion from visible/infrared into microwaves.

  • Predicted by Alpher and Herman in 1948 (they estimated ~5 K).
  • Found by accident in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson as unremovable noise in a horn antenna.
  • Measured at 2.725 K, arriving almost equally from every direction, with a near-perfect black-body spectrum (COBE, 1990).
  • Tiny ripples of about 1 part in 100 000 are the seeds that grew into galaxies (WMAP, Planck).

The Steady State model has no hot dense phase, so it has nothing to make this radiation. Hoyle proposed that starlight was thermalised by iron "whiskers" in space — but that cannot produce a black-body spectrum this perfect or a glow this smooth.

2 · The abundance of light elements

About 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass is found almost everywhere, even in the oldest, least-processed stars. Big Bang nucleosynthesis produces exactly this in the first few minutes. Stars fusing hydrogen over billions of years cannot account for that much helium — this was the problem Steady State could never solve, and ironically Hoyle himself worked out much of the stellar nucleosynthesis that demonstrated it.

3 · The distant universe looks different

Looking far away is looking back in time. Distant regions contain more quasars, more radio-loud galaxies and more small, blue, irregular galaxies than the nearby universe. Martin Ryle's radio source counts in the 1950s–60s showed the density of sources was higher in the past. A Steady State universe must look statistically identical at all times and places — the "perfect cosmological principle" — so this observation alone falsifies it.

Beyond the balloon

The balloon is a good first analogy and a bad second one. Here it is running next to the real model, so you can see precisely which features carry over and which ones mislead.

The balloon analogy
Inflate×1.00

What the balloon gets right

  • Every dot moves away from every other dot — no dot is the centre.
  • Dots twice as far apart separate twice as fast: Hubble's law falls out naturally.
  • The surface has no edge, yet it has finite area.
  • The dots do not travel across the rubber — the rubber between them stretches.

Where the balloon breaks down

  • The balloon has a centre — but it is inside the rubber, in a dimension the "universe" (the surface) does not contain. Students look at the picture and see a centre anyway.
  • It appears to expand into the surrounding room. Real space needs no outside to expand into; expansion means distances within it grow, not that it invades somewhere else.
  • The universe is 3D, not a 2D surface — and may be infinite, in which case there is no "whole balloon" to picture from outside at all.
  • Drawn dots stretch as the rubber stretches. Real galaxies do not: they are held together by gravity and simply do not grow. Use stuck-on sequins or coins, not pen marks.
  • It suggests a rubber sheet is being pulled by something. Nothing pulls space; the expansion rate is set by the energy content of the universe itself.
  • It gives no sense of time, temperature or density — the very things that distinguish Big Bang from Steady State.
Better classroom follow-up Stretch a length of elastic with beads glued on, or use the "raisin bread" loaf: raisins separate as the dough rises, the raisins themselves do not swell, and a raisin twice as far away recedes twice as fast — with no special raisin anywhere.
Part 6 — Fixing the wiring

Six misconceptions worth unlearning

Common answer

"The Big Bang was an explosion that threw matter outwards into empty space."

What is actually going on

An explosion has a centre, an edge and a surrounding space to expand into. The Big Bang had none of these. It was not an event in space — it was the beginning of space and time, happening at every point simultaneously. Matter is not moving outwards; the separations between matter are growing.

Common answer

"Everything is moving away from us, so we must be near the centre."

What is actually going on

Every observer in the universe sees exactly the same thing — try Part 2 above. Uniform expansion looks identical from every vantage point, so "everything recedes from me" is evidence of homogeneity, not of a special location.

Common answer

"The universe is expanding into the space around it."

What is actually going on

There is no "around it". Expansion is a statement about the internal geometry — the distance between any two widely separated galaxies increases with time. If the universe is infinite it was always infinite; it just has more distance in it now.

Common answer

"If space expands, then I am expanding, and so is the ruler I measure with."

What is actually going on

Gravitationally and electromagnetically bound systems do not expand: atoms, you, the Earth, the Solar System, the Milky Way, even the Local Group. Their internal forces hold them at a fixed size while the space between distant clusters grows. That is precisely why we can measure the expansion at all — our rulers stay the same length.

Common answer

"Redshift proves the Big Bang."

What is actually going on

Redshift proves expansion. The Steady State model was fully expanding and fully consistent with Hubble's law. The Big Bang wins because of the CMB, the helium abundance, and the observed evolution of galaxies — evidence about the universe's history, not just its motion.

Common answer

"Redshift is the Doppler effect — galaxies are rushing away through space."

What is actually going on

At GCSE, treating it as a Doppler-like shift is accepted and gives the right answer for nearby galaxies. Strictly, cosmological redshift is different: the wave is stretched while in flight because the space it is travelling through expands. That is why 1 + z = anow/aemitted, and why very distant galaxies can have recession speeds greater than c without anything moving through space faster than light.

The one-paragraph answer Distant galaxies are redshifted, and the further away they are the greater the redshift, so the universe is expanding. Running that expansion backwards implies everything was once hot, dense and close together — the Big Bang. The strongest evidence is the cosmic microwave background: radiation released about 380 000 years after the beginning, now stretched to microwaves at 2.7 K and arriving from every direction. The rival Steady State theory also had an expanding universe, but with matter continuously created so that density stayed constant; it was rejected because it predicts no microwave background, cannot explain the helium abundance, and requires the distant universe to look the same as the nearby one, which it does not.
Part 8 — Check yourself

Four questions

Modelling notes. The simulation is a 2-dimensional, deliberately simplified model. The Big Bang panel uses a flat, matter-dominated expansion, a(t) ∝ t2/3, scaled so that the present age reads 13.8 billion years; the real universe also contains dark energy, which makes the expansion accelerate. The Steady State panel uses exponential expansion, a ∝ eHt, with new galaxies created to hold the number density constant. Number density is shown per unit area rather than per unit volume, so on-screen counts scale as a² rather than a³ — the density readout uses the correct 3D scaling. H0 is taken as 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.
