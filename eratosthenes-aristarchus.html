Key ideas (3.3)

    
Eratosthenes & the Earth
At noon on the solstice the Sun is overhead at Syene (no shadow) but casts a 7.2° shadow at Alexandria. Because the Sun's rays are parallel, that shadow angle equals the angle at the Earth's centre between the two cities. 7.2° is 1/50 of a circle, so the whole circumference is 50 × the 800 km between them = 40,000 km.

    
Aristarchus & the Moon
A lunar eclipse shows the Moon is roughly a quarter of the Earth's width. Its angular size (½°) then gives its distance, since a small object of known size at a known angle sits at a fixed distance.

    
Aristarchus & the Sun
At exactly half-Moon the Sun–Moon–Earth angle is 90°. Measuring the Earth's angle between the Moon and Sun gives the Sun's distance. Because the true angle (89.85°) is so close to 90°, Aristarchus's estimate of 87° made the Sun come out ~20× too close — but his method was correct.

    
Why it matters
These measurements, made over 2000 years ago with shadows and geometry, first revealed the true scale of the Solar System — that the Sun is far larger than the Earth, which supported the later idea that the Earth orbits the Sun.
