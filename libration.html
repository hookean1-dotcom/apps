Key ideas (2.7 & 2.8)

Synchronous rotation (2.7)

The Moon turns on its axis in exactly the same time it takes to orbit the Earth (27.3 days). So the same face always points towards us — we see the near side and never the far side.

What is libration?

Even though it keeps one face towards us, the Moon appears to slowly rock and nod. This wobble is called libration, and it lets us peek a little way around the edges — so over time we see about 59% of the surface, not just 50%.

Cause 1 — libration in longitude

The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so it speeds up and slows down (fastest at perigee). But its spin is steady. Sometimes the spin gets ahead of the orbit and sometimes behind, so we peek around the east and west edges — up to ±7.9°.

Cause 2 — libration in latitude

The Moon's spin axis is tilted about 6.7° to its orbit. So as it goes round, we sometimes look over its north pole and sometimes over its south pole — up to ±6.7°.

The effect (2.8)

Libration reveals thin zones all around the limb of the Moon. Adding these to the 50% near side brings the total ever visible to about 59%.
