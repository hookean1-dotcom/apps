How small is an arcsecond? (13.10)

A full circle is 360°. Each degree splits into 60 arcminutes, and each arcminute into 60 arcseconds. So:
1° = 60′ = 3600″
One arcsecond is tiny — about the width of a £1 coin seen from 4.5 km away. Even the nearest star wobbles by less than one arcsecond, which is why measuring parallax is so hard.

Key ideas

What is parallax?

When you look at a nearby object from two different places, it seems to shift against the faraway background. That apparent shift is parallax. The closer the object, the bigger the shift.

How we use it for stars (13.12)

As the Earth orbits the Sun, we see a nearby star from opposite sides of our orbit (six months apart). The star appears to shift against the distant stars. The parallax angle p is half of that total shift.

The parsec (13.11)

A parsec is the distance at which a star would have a parallax angle of exactly 1 arcsecond. Then distance and angle are simply linked: d (parsec) = 1 ÷ p (arcseconds). One parsec is about 3.26 light years.

Why parallax has a limit

Far-away stars wobble by less than a tiny fraction of an arcsecond — too small to measure, even from space. Beyond a few thousand parsecs we need other methods.

The distance ladder

Parallax measures nearby stars. Those are used to work out the true brightness of special pulsing stars (Cepheids), which reach nearby galaxies. Those calibrate exploding stars and Hubble's law, which reach the farthest galaxies. Each rung is built on the one below.
