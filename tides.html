Why two bulges?
The Moon's gravity is strongest on the near side, weaker at the centre, and weakest on the far side (gravity falls off with distance). Compared with the pull on the Earth's centre, the near-side ocean is pulled towards the Moon and the far-side ocean is left behind — so a bulge of water forms on both sides. As the Earth spins under these two bulges, every coast gets two high tides each day.

Key ideas (3.5)

What causes the tides?

The gravitational pull of the Moon (helped by the Sun) on the Earth's oceans. Because gravity is stronger on the side nearest the Moon and weaker on the far side, the water is stretched into two bulges — one facing the Moon, one opposite.

Why two high tides a day?

There are two bulges, on opposite sides of the Earth. As the Earth rotates once, every place passes through both bulges — so it has two high tides and two low tides, about 12 h 25 min apart.

Spring tides

At new Moon and full Moon the Sun, Earth and Moon are in a line, so the Sun's and Moon's tides add together. This gives the highest highs and lowest lows — the largest tidal range. ("Spring" means to leap up — nothing to do with the season.)

Neap tides

At the first and third quarter the Sun and Moon are at right angles, so their tides partly cancel. This gives the smallest tidal range — modest highs and lows.

Why the Moon beats the Sun

Tidal force depends on mass ÷ distance³. The Sun's huge mass is outweighed by its huge distance, so its tidal effect is only about 46% of the Moon's. The Moon is the main tide-raiser.
